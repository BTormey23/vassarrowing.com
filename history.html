History

Rowing at Vassar, and the water we row on

Vassar students founded a rowing club in 1866, and today's crews train on the same stretch of the Hudson where, for half a century, the Poughkeepsie Regatta drew national crowds. This is the story as far as we can document it, and an invitation to help us fill in the rest.

Vassar's first sport

Rowing at Vassar is older than almost anything else in the College's athletic life. According to the Vassar Encyclopedia, in 1866 students founded croquet, archery and rowing clubs, at a time when the College encouraged exercise but, in the Encyclopedia's words, "doubted the merits of competition." Matthew Vassar believed physical activity would bring balance to his students' lives.

That early rowing happened on campus water, not on the river. Writing in Scribner's Magazine in 1898, Margaret Pollock Sherwood wrote that the college girl "is roused to physical activity. There is rowing on the lake." The Hudson was part of student life too, through ferry crossings and river excursions, but she was plain about the rules: "Rowing on the river is not permitted." It would take most of another century for Vassar crews to make the Hudson their home.

A program is born on the Hudson

Vassar became coeducational in 1969. The modern rowing program came later. As Vassar Athletics tells it, "Rowing at Vassar began with one young coach, two old wooden shells, and a handful of students who were eager to spend some time on the Hudson River." That was the fall of 1983, and the founder was Scott Sanford, then principal of Spackenkill High School, coaching part-time. He called rowing "the champagne of sports." Ed Clark helped by coaching the women's squad.

The first crews rowed out of F.D. Roosevelt High School's boathouse in two donated wooden shells. As the team grew, A.C. Dutton Lumber Corp. donated a small space next to the Marist College boathouse, about 2,000 meters downriver, and Sanford and his rowers built their own docks. In 1994–95, under President Frances Fergusson, the College acquired adjacent riverfront property with three buildings to renovate. Today's facilities include the boathouse, a clubhouse used for video review, and a launch house.

Varsity years and beyond

Rowing earned varsity status in 1997. Head Coach Mike Alton arrived in 2000 and turned the program's focus toward eights. The results followed: a third-place varsity eight at the 2001 ECAC Championships and a national top-20 ranking, a win in the club eight at the 2002 Head of the Schuylkill, and in 2004 a sixth-place finish for the women's varsity eight at ECACs, which Vassar Athletics called the highest eights placement in program history. Later headlines include a men's double that medaled at the 2012 Head of the Charles as the fastest undergraduate boat in its event.

In the fall of 2010 rowing moved to club status, though the College's sesquicentennial history notes it "was retained within the athletics program." Today Peter Wells coaches both the men's and women's squads. He came to Vassar in November 2018 after 35 years as head men's coach at Williams College, and was named head coach in June 2019. Athletics materials also name Rodney Mott and Stephanie Ricker among past head coaches; we do not yet have a complete list, and we would like one.

The Poughkeepsie Regatta

Long before Vassar put a shell on the river, this stretch of the Hudson was the center of American rowing. The Intercollegiate Rowing Association was founded in 1891 by Cornell, Columbia and Penn, and on June 24, 1895, it held its first championship regatta at Poughkeepsie. Marist's archivists explain why the site was chosen: a relatively straight four-mile stretch of river, sloping banks that let spectators see the race, room to run an observation train along the course, and neutral water that belonged to no one school. The varsity race was four miles.

The event became known simply as the Poughkeepsie Regatta, and it grew into a national spectacle. By 1899 the observation train that followed the race along the west shore, "a moving grandstand" of flatcars fitted with seats, ran to 48 cars. Tens of thousands of spectators came to town each June, hundreds of boats and yachts moored along the river, and the crowds included Franklin and Eleanor Roosevelt, Theodore Roosevelt, J. Pierpont Morgan, the Astors and the Vanderbilts. Poughkeepsie and Highland, on opposite banks, housed the crews in homes, boarding houses, schools and hotels.

The course and its landmarks

Crews raced south toward Poughkeepsie from the Hyde Park end, near today's Culinary Institute of America campus. Accounts differ on the finish line: Scenic Hudson places it at the Mid-Hudson Bridge, while other accounts place it at the railroad bridge that is now the Walkway Over the Hudson. The railroad bridge was completed on January 1, 1889, and after a 1974 fire it reopened in 2009 as a pedestrian park, 212 feet above the river. The Mid-Hudson Bridge opened on August 25, 1930, with then-Governor Franklin Roosevelt and Eleanor Roosevelt at the ceremony.

Regatta Row

For years, crews stored their boats in long wooden plank sheds built by local citizens; none of those survive. Between 1931 and 1938 the Poughkeepsie Regatta Association, with city support, built permanent boathouses on what became known as "Regatta Row," off Waterworks Road in the Town of Poughkeepsie: the MIT/Washington boathouse (1931) and dining hall (1936), the California boathouse (1932), and the Cornell boathouse, completed in June 1938 as a WPA project. The MIT/Washington buildings burned in 1957, and the California boathouse came down in the late 1970s. The Cornell boathouse is the only one left.

1936: the Boys in the Boat

On Monday evening, June 22, 1936, the University of Washington won the freshman, junior varsity and varsity races at Poughkeepsie, a sweep no school had managed since Cornell in 1912. The varsity eight covered the four miles in 19:09 3/5. On July 5 the same crew won the Olympic trials on Lake Carnegie at Princeton, and on August 14 in Berlin it took the gold medal, finishing ahead of Italy and Germany with the top three boats separated by about a second.

That crew, and oarsman Joe Rantz in particular, became the subject of Daniel James Brown's 2013 book The Boys in the Boat and of George Clooney's 2023 film. Vassar crews train today on the same water where that season was won.

Why the regatta left

The regatta was interrupted by both World Wars and skipped 1933 during the Depression. It returned after World War II with parades and a regatta queen, but, as Marist's archivists put it, the celebrations "did not meet the crews' needs for additional boathouses and more." After 1949 the IRA left Poughkeepsie for Marietta, Ohio, in 1950, and then Syracuse. Rowing on this reach of the river did not stop: local high school crews began racing here in 1950, and the Mid-Hudson Rowing Association ran the Mid-Hudson Invitational Regatta from 1950 to 2000.

Keepers of the history

In 1976 the Poughkeepsie Journal drew attention to the condition of the surviving Regatta Row boathouses, and Marist stepped in to care for them. Marist restored the Cornell boathouse in 2009, and it now sits in Longview Park as an event space and overflow shell storage. The Quadricentennial regatta that year brought crews including Columbia, Cornell, Navy, Penn, Syracuse, Army, Marist and Vassar back to the historic water.

Marist's Archives and Special Collections also maintains the Poughkeepsie Regatta Collection. It began in the early 2000s, during the boathouse restoration, with a few programs, artifacts and news articles, and has grown since 2013 through newspaper research, images, film footage and donated items. Marist has put part of it online as an exhibit, which is the best place to start for anyone who wants to see what race day looked like.

Our boathouse today

Vassar's boathouse sits on North Water Street, next door to the Hudson River Rowing Association's community boathouse. The HRRA was formed by local high school coaches, who worked with Marist, Vassar, Dutchess County, the City of Poughkeepsie and the Mid-Hudson Rowing Association to build a boathouse for scholastic and adult rowing. It opened in 2006 and today is home to eight regional rowing programs, run by volunteers.

The history is close at hand. In September 2025, Vassar's Alum Rowing Regatta and Celebration Weekend honored Joe Rantz, with his daughter Judy Willman and granddaughter Jennifer Huffman as guests. Jennifer rowed twice that weekend with Vassar students and alumni. "My hope was to have Jennifer row on the water her grandpa did," Coach Wells said. "For me, there's no place in the U.S.—and maybe the world—more sacred to rowing than here in Poughkeepsie." In recent years HRRA and the Mid-Hudson Rowing Association have also brought back a Poughkeepsie Regatta for local crews, and Vassar crews have raced in it.

What comes next

Vassar rowing's own history is still mostly unwritten. We know the outline, but not the crews, the coaches in every era, the names on the old shells, or the photographs sitting in alumni closets. We would like to gather those stories and images in one place, and in time help build something larger: a shared record of rowing on this water, from the Regatta Row era to the present, perhaps one day a rowing history museum for the Mid-Hudson. That starts with what you remember.
